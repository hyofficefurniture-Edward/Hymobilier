Hotel headboard wall: power outlets, reading lights and wall protection

Mounting heights for outlets and reading lights, bedside control, wall-protection finishes and plan-level coordination: the decisions that make a hotel headboard wall work from the first night of operation.

Reference image generated for the group's internal library, no brand and no named project. Visual reference, not an announcement of a delivery in any specific country.

The headboard wall concentrates, on a few square metres, most of the rework reported after a guest room opens: an outlet nobody can reach, a reading lamp aimed at the reader's face, a finish marked by rolled headboards. The cause is almost never the furniture itself; it is late coordination between the electrical plan, the furniture shop drawings and the choice of finishes. This article sets out the decisions to settle on plan, in which order, and which documents to freeze before ordering.

Why is the headboard wall decided before the bed is chosen?

In a hotel guest room the headboard wall is not decoration; it is an interface. On one side sit joinery and upholstery, on the other electrical points, lighting control and finishes that must survive daily use. Until the bed width, the nightstand positions and the headboard overhang are fixed, outlet and lamp positions remain hanging. If the electrician works ahead of the shop drawings, points end up behind solid panels or, worse, inside the volume a nightstand hides. The correct order is therefore: room layout frozen, wall shop drawings approved, then electrical points transferred onto the wall elevation. That sequence removes most rework and applies to renovation as much as to new build.

At what height should outlets and reading lights be mounted?

Usual heights are settled on plan, together with the electrician and the architect, from three references: finished floor level, the top of the mattress or made-up bed, and the top of the nightstand. An outlet set too low becomes unreachable once the headboard is installed; a lamp set too high lights the reader's face instead of the page. The reliable method is to draw the wall at true scale, trace a seated reader on it, and place every point relative to that silhouette rather than to the bare wall. Each project then fixes its own dimensions; they are frozen on the execution drawing and checked on the mock-up before series production.

Reading lights: what aiming and what control?

A hotel reading lamp is judged on three things: beam aim, control, and independence of each side. The beam must light the page without crossing the bed axis; it is the lamp body orientation, more than its output, that decides comfort. Control must be reachable without sitting up, from the seated position, and independent of the room's master switch. Finally, each side of the bed is switched separately: two guests do not read at the same time. On plan these requirements translate into one technical sheet per luminaire and one control point per side, transferred onto the wall elevation together with the furniture shop drawings.

Wall protection: what should be specified above and around the bed?

The most stressed band of the wall is the one no render shows: the strip where heads rest, where luggage leans during housekeeping, where the bed runner rubs at every turn-down. Finishes there are chosen to be washable and repairable: lacquered timber panels or laminates for the frame, coated technical fabrics for soft surfaces, fold-down protection behind the headboard where connecting rooms are planned. The specification separates the contact zone, which receives the toughest finish, from the upper bands, which can be more decorative. These zones are defined line by line on the elevation, and every finish is validated by an approved sample against a document.

How is the wall coordinated with the furniture on plan?

Coordination happens on a single view: the headboard wall elevation, on which the headboard, nightstands, electrical points and lamps are overlaid. Three checks are systematic. First, does each outlet axis fall outside the volume hidden by the headboard and outside the volume occupied by the nightstand? Second, does the nightstand top edge leave the outlet reachable without moving furniture? Third, have the joinery installation tolerances (a few millimetres per panel) absorbed the allowance needed for cable routes? This one view, signed by both the furniture and electrical packages before ordering, replaces weeks of email loops during installation.

Which documents should be frozen before ordering?

Four documents are enough to secure a headboard wall. The dimensioned architectural plan of the room, which provides the height references. The coordinated electrical plan, where every point is numbered and transferred onto the wall elevation. The furniture shop drawings with dimensioned sections, which show what the joinery hides and what it leaves accessible. And the sign-off report of the mock-up, which validates a sample room in the workshop or on site before series production. As a space reference, a hotel project in Zanzibar (Tanzania) documents guest rooms whose headboard walls combine timber panels, wall lamps and floating nightstands; those images serve to discuss compositions, they do not describe a delivery in any specific country and involve no brand.
