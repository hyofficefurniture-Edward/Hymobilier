PRACTICAL GUIDE

How many covers to plan for before ordering restaurant furniture

Turning restaurant floor area into a cover count: deductions, table formats, aisle widths, service style and a zone-by-zone seating table.

Image from the TOA Hotel & Spa Zanzibar (Tanzania) project file. A space reference, not an announcement of a delivery in France or Morocco.

The cover count is settled before the tables are chosen, not after. A room sized by eye and then furnished often ends up with aisles that are too tight or rows that cannot be sold, and a layout is hard to correct once the furniture is ordered. Area, deductions, table formats and circulation turn into a simple schedule that every proposal can follow and challenge. The aim is not to fill the room, but to reach a cover count that holds up in service. Here is how to move from floor area to a number of covers, and which margins to check on site before the order is fixed.

Start from usable seating area, not total floor area

A 180 m² dining room does not offer 180 m² of seats. Deduct the bar, the buffet, service stations, exits and circulation first, then apply 1.2 to 1.8 m² per cover for table service, or 0.9 to 1.2 m² for quick service and cafés. These figures are planning references, not a delivery commitment: room shape, columns, banquettes and the chosen furniture all change the result. For example, 180 m² less 25 m² of bar, 18 m² of buffet and 20 per cent circulation leaves about 110 m² of seating, or 60 to 90 covers depending on the comfort level sought. Keep the density consistent with the service you advertise, and calculate a terrace separately.

Choose table formats that can be combined

A 700 to 800 mm square table seats two; two of them pushed together make a four, provided the bases do not clash and the tops line up. A 1200 to 1400 mm by 800 mm rectangular table does the same job without handling. A round table of 1200 to 1400 mm in diameter seats four to six and softens the corners of a room. A long table suits groups and narrow rooms. Check the height under the top, usually 720 to 750 mm, and the footprint of the bases, which decides real legroom. Mixing formats only helps if heights, edges and finishes stay compatible, and if a split pair does not block an aisle.

Set the aisles and the clearances

Three widths structure the room: 1200 mm for the main aisle, 900 mm for the service aisle, and 900 mm between the table edge and the wall so a chair can be pulled out. Between two occupied tables, allow 900 to 1000 mm from chair back to chair back; below that, guests rise sideways and service slows down. These are working minimums: check them on a scaled plan with the actual furniture, and against the accessibility and fire safety rules that apply to the premises. Add fixed obstacles — columns, radiators, doors — before counting. A 1200 mm aisle on paper can become 600 mm once chairs are pulled out.

Service style changes the arithmetic

Plated service holds a table for longer than a buffet or a counter does. The cover count therefore cannot be read from floor area alone: it depends on how long guests stay and how many services you run. Estimate turnover per service, then check that the kitchen and bar can keep up; adding covers without extra kitchen throughput simply moves the wait into the dining room. Bar seating counts differently: a stool takes less floor than a table but stays occupied for longer. An all-day lobby bar is not sized like a dinner room. Count standing places and stools as separate categories, not as tables, and note that a counter turns faster at lunch than at dinner.

Terrace, breakfast and the seating table

On a terrace and at breakfast, furniture has to move, stack and withstand exposure. Allow a store or a corner for tables that are not in use, and check the weight of each piece if staff handle it alone. Anticipate wind, sun and rain, which drive the choice of materials and the storage arrangement. At breakfast, density can be higher and occupancy shorter. Then set out a table by zone: gross area, deductions, table formats, resulting covers. That table becomes the reference document for comparing proposals; the five points in the checklist still need confirming on site. Loose furniture also makes cleaning and evening re-sets easier.

Frequently asked questions

How much floor area should I allow per cover?

Allow 1.2 to 1.8 m² per cover for table service and 0.9 to 1.2 m² for quick service. Apply these densities to usable seating area, after deducting the bar, buffet and circulation, and check them on a scaled plan.

What is the minimum aisle width in a restaurant?

Allow 1200 mm for the main aisle and 900 mm for the service aisle. Between two occupied tables, keep 900 to 1000 mm from chair back to chair back, then measure again with chairs pulled out.

Are round tables better than square tables?

Neither is better in every room. A 700 to 800 mm square table seats two and pairs into a four; a round table of 1200 to 1400 mm seats four to six and softens corners. Mix them only if heights and finishes match.

Should terrace seats be included in the cover count?

Yes, but as a separate figure. A terrace has its own area, furniture and storage needs, because wind, rain and sun require pieces that move and stack. Add the two zones only after checking each layout.

When should the furniture specification be fixed?

After the zone table is complete, not before. Usable area, deductions, table formats and aisle widths need to be settled and checked on site, because a layout is hard to correct once the furniture is ordered.
