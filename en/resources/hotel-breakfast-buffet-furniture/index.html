PRACTICAL GUIDE

Hotel breakfast buffet furniture: heights, flow, holding

Counter height, aisle width, station counts and hot holding: the dimensions that decide whether a hotel breakfast buffet still flows at 8 a.m.

Reference image from the group library, no brand and no named project. Visual reference, not an announcement of a delivery in any specific country.

A breakfast buffet is judged at eight in the morning, when 150 guests arrive within half an hour. At that moment the dimensions do the talking: worktop height, aisle width, line length, socket positions. Hotel breakfast buffet furniture is a technical package, and the buffet counter should be fixed on plan before tender rather than chosen from a catalogue after the floor is laid.

What height should a hotel buffet counter be?

The reference height is 900 mm from finished floor to the top of the worktop. That puts the surface at forearm level for a standing adult and lets guests serve with tongs without bending. Below about 850 mm, deep gastronorm pans force a stoop; above about 950 mm, guests can no longer see the bottom of the pan. Depth typically holds at 700 mm on a single-sided counter: roughly 400 mm for the pan, 250 mm of landing space for the plate, 50 mm overhang. Below about 650 mm, a loaded plate tips at the edge. A double-sided island keeps 900 mm in height but goes to 900 mm in depth, with equipment down the centre. The accessible section typically drops to 850 mm, with 700 mm of clear height under the worktop over 600 mm of depth. Place it at the end of the line, never in the middle, where a wheelchair would block the flow as effectively as a column.

How wide should the aisle in front of a buffet be?

Allow 1,200 mm in front of a single-flow buffet, 1,500 mm where two flows cross, and 1,800 mm at the ends and in front of live cooking. Measure from the counter edge to the first real obstruction: chair back, column, wall. At about 900 mm a guest passes alone: to let someone by, a guest has to set the tray on the counter, which stops service. At 1,200 mm two guests pass if one carries a tray. The 1,500 mm figure is needed at the coffee machine and at the corner of an L-shaped buffet, where the queue compresses. One thing is usually missing from the drawing: the queue itself. A standing guest takes about 600 mm of line, so ten people waiting occupy 6 m. If the first pan sits 2 m from the door, the queue blocks the entrance.

How many buffet stations for 150 covers?

For 150 breakfast covers, plan three service stations and one beverage station. The number comes from throughput, not floor area: a 2.4 m station serves 45-60 guests an hour at peak, and breakfast service concentrates into 60-75 minutes on most weekdays. The cold station runs at 50-60 guests an hour: little time is spent there, but people cross. The hot station drops to 45-55, because guests hesitate and set the tongs down. The beverage station absorbs 70-80 passes an hour over 1.8 m: duplicate it rather than lengthen it. Total line length lands around 8.4 m for four stations, half of which is taken by refrigeration packs rather than landing space.

How do you integrate hot holding?

Hot holding sits at 63 °C minimum at the core of the product and cold holding at 4 °C maximum, thresholds used across EU food hygiene practice. A decorative pan sitting at 55 °C is a risk pan, however well integrated. The bain-marie is forgiving and cheap to run, but it humidifies food. An induction warmer heats the pan directly: drier and more responsive, but it needs a compatible pan and a probe. A forced-air heated display suits pastries, not sauces. Cold holding follows the same logic. A refrigerated display sheds heat at the back: built into a closed unit without low and high ventilation, it climbs in temperature. On a 700 mm counter, reserve at least 100 mm at the rear for airflow. One equipped station draws 3-6 kW; four stations typically represent 16-24 kW, split across 16 A circuits with 30 mA protection, using IP44 sockets 300 mm above the floor, inside the unit, behind a removable panel.

How should cold, hot and beverage zones be arranged?

The sequence follows the guest's movement, not the menu: plate and cutlery, cold, bread, hot, beverages, exit. That order prevents guests doubling back with a loaded tray, and it can be checked on the drawing before the room opens to guests. Cold goes first in the line: it tolerates waiting and serves quickly. Bread follows, provided it is replenished from the kitchen side: an empty basket on the guest side forces staff to cross the line. Hot comes next, with eggs last if a live-cooking post exists. Beverages are duplicated at both ends, or isolated on a dedicated station: it is the only zone guests return to. On an island, the exit must be physically separate from the entry, otherwise the unit becomes a funnel from 80 covers. The clearing station sits opposite, on its own service route, away from guests.

What seat density works for a breakfast room?

A breakfast room runs at 1.2-1.4 m² per seat, circulation included, against 1.5-1.8 m² for a table-service restaurant. The tighter figure works because the meal is short, and it is checked on the plan, never estimated by eye on site. A 700 × 700 mm table typically seats two, a 1,200 × 700 mm table seats four. Keep at least 900 mm between tables and 600 mm of chair pull-out: below that, a guest cannot leave without pushing the chair into the aisle. A continuous banquette saves the pull-out on one side but fixes the seat at 450 mm. Furniture also has to survive service: ABS edging and removable covers outlast a façade finish. The handling constraints are the same as those for modular furniture in a dining room that changes layout between services.

What checks should be done before signing off the plan?

Four simple checks before ordering: mark the counter footprint and aisle widths on the floor at full scale, verify clear height with the actual equipment, position each socket against the appliance it feeds, and confirm module access to the room. Marking out with tape reveals what a 2D drawing does not: a column in the aisle, a door opening into the flow, a floor step at the entrance. Module access is checked against the service lift and kitchen doors: a 2.4 m counter ships in 1.2 m modules. Finish at the food contact matters too: smooth demountable joints, radiused internal corners. Outdoors, the same rules apply with different materials, as our guide to hotel terrace and poolside furniture sets out; indoors, the logic matches that of hotel lobby circulation zones. Hongye Furniture Group builds these counters on its joinery and metal lines, with dimensional checks at the end of the line. Quality management follows ISO 9001, food hygiene principles follow the Codex Alimentarius, equipment hygiene requirements follow NF EN 1672-2 as published by AFNOR, and contract furniture durability follows BIFMA references.

Frequently asked questions

Can a breakfast buffet be installed on carpet?

No, not on the service zone. Allow a hard floor strip of at least 1.5 m in front of the counter, for spills, crumbs and trolley traffic. Carpet can stay on the seating side if the circulation path is properly finished.

Does a hot buffet need an extraction hood?

Only above live cooking. A bain-marie and a heated display give off steam, but general room extract copes with that load without extra ducting. An omelette station does need a dedicated hood above it and a supply of fresh air.

What height should an accessible buffet section be?

850 mm to the worktop, with 700 mm of clear height underneath and 600 mm of depth, over at least 900 mm of width. Put it at the end of the line, never in the middle, where a parked wheelchair would block other guests.

How many sockets per station?

Two to three dedicated circuits per equipped station, on 16 A with 30 mA protection, using IP44 sockets 300 mm above the floor behind a removable panel. Keep hot holding and beverage circuits separate: a coffee machine will not tolerate a bain-marie's load.

Can existing furniture be reused for a new buffet?

Rarely without checks: worktop height, flatness over 3 m, clear height underneath and ventilation for refrigeration packs. An 850 mm unit suits an accessible section, not a hot line, where deep pans and a bain-marie need a 900 mm worktop.

How do you avoid a queue at the coffee machine?

Duplicate the coffee point at both ends and keep 1,500 mm clear in front of each machine. It is the only position guests return to, often twice, so it generates most of the crossings in the room during the breakfast peak, more than the hot line.

What lead time for a custom buffet counter?

Typically 45-60 days of production after the drawing and finish sample are approved, plus shipping and installation; this range is indicative and is confirmed in the proposal. Above 2.4 m, a route survey is needed before fabrication and site assembly adds a full day to the installation programme on site.
