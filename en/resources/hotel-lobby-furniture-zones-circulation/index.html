Hotel Lobby Furniture: Zones, Circulation and Selection

Five zones, measured clear widths and fabrics chosen by use: how to furnish a hotel lobby without over-specifying the quiet corners or under-specifying the bar.

Image from the group library, taken from a hotel project in Zanzibar (Tanzania). A space reference for composition and fixing details, not an announcement of a delivery in France or Morocco.

A lobby is not a room furnished in one gesture. It is a sequence of moments a guest crosses in seconds, suitcase in hand. Reception, waiting, work, bar and the circulation spine share neither the same clear widths, nor the same fabrics, nor the same service life. The markers below are ranges seen in hotel work and are to be confirmed on plan and against supplier documentation. They are there to frame the right questions before comparing proposals, not to rank a supplier.

Read the lobby as five zones, not one room

A lobby is read on foot, not from a plan. From the entrance to the desk, then from the desk to the lifts, a guest passes through at least four atmospheres in a few seconds: he puts down a case, looks for a reference point, waits, sometimes sits, orders a coffee, leaves. Each moment calls for different furniture and, above all, a different clear width. Splitting the lobby into five zones — arrival, waiting, work and social, bar, circulation spine — stops the room being treated as one batch of seating. The method needs three surveys. First the spine: the most direct line between entrance, reception and lifts, kept at 1.20 m clear minimum, widening to 1.50 m at crossings and in front of the queue. Then the contact points: hands on armrests, cases against benches, trays set on low tables. Finally the dimensions of each group, taken between furniture rather than between walls, because a chair pushed back 20 cm eats a passage. That split has a direct consequence for the tender pack. A lobby specified with one fabric and one foam density will be over-specified in the quiet corners and under-specified around the bar. Separating the zones lets each carry its own rub count, its own cover type and its own replacement logic, without paying twice for the same performance.

Arrival and reception: the first three metres

The arrival zone carries the hardest-working furniture in the lobby: reception desk, counter return, low console, luggage bench, high stools for standing waits, umbrella stand. The worktop usually sits between 1,050 and 1,150 mm, with an accessible position at 750–800 mm, 700 mm clear knee width and 450 mm knee depth. In front of the desk, allow 1,200 mm clear for a guest with a case and a companion; behind it, 900 mm for the chair and staff movement. A luggage bench sits at 450–500 mm seat height, 400–450 mm deep, with a top protected by an applied edge. The desk front takes trolleys, cases and belt buckles. A high-pressure laminate face or a veneer with 2 mm ABS edging can be repaired locally; a lacquer far less so. Treat the plinth as a wear part: removable kick plate, through-bolted fixings, a colour close to the floor to hide scuffs. High stools in the waiting position need vinyl at 50,000 Martindale minimum, a seat with no seam across the front, and replaceable glides. One detail is often missed: the queue. Without a floor line or a drop-off bench, guests stop at the desk and block the spine. A waiting seat 1,500 mm from the desk, or a 400 mm deep console for bags, often clears it. Add a socket near the bench and lighting that makes the desk readable without glare in the eyes of someone seated.

Waiting: set the spacing before choosing the chair

In the waiting zone, comfort comes down to simple dimensions. A lobby armchair sits at 420–450 mm, is 480–520 mm deep and allows 600–700 mm width per place. Between two chairs, a 400–450 mm diameter side table needs 400–450 mm clear on each side. Two chairs facing each other sit 800–900 mm apart so knees do not touch; behind the backs, allow 1,200 mm where the circulation spine runs. Check these on a 1:20 plan, not on a mood image. Waiting upholstery works through prolonged contact rather than hard abrasion: 40,000 to 50,000 Martindale for a lobby seat, 25,000 to 30,000 for a back. Seat foam at 35–40 kg/m³ holds its shape; below that the seat compacts and the front edge collapses. A removable cover with a concealed closure and the pile running the right way lets a cushion be turned or replaced without taking the group out of service. A pale velvet soils at the back and armrests: choose a mid-tone or a tight weave. The real cost of lobby seating is not read at order stage but at the first replacement. Three decisions reduce it: cushions independent of the back, covers available separately, a demountable frame. In use, cushions turn, covers wash, a frame is repaired without scrapping the seat. A fully upholstered and glued seat, by contrast, forces the whole chair out for one stain or one local collapse.

Work and social: a table that takes a laptop

The work-and-social zone now hosts mixed uses: a guest answering messages, two colleagues preparing a meeting, a traveller waiting for a taxi. A bar-height table does not work for seated laptop use; 740–750 mm height, 600 mm usable depth per person and 700 mm seat width remain the baseline. For a shared table, allow 600 mm width per place and 1,200 mm between back-to-back rows once chairs are pushed back. A fixed banquette saves space but requires the seat depth (450–480 mm) and the table clearance (250–300 mm) to be calculated. Power points decide the real use. Two sockets per table, one of them USB-C, with a cable route cut at machining stage rather than drilled on site, remove visible extension leads. The top should stay matt under lighting: a glossy laminate throws the screen back. On acoustics, a stone-and-glass lobby sends conversation towards reception; absorbent panels under table tops or inside seats, plus heavy textiles, lower the level without dividing the room. In the social zone, table edges, armrests and backs wear first. A 2 mm edge, a top replaceable independently of the base and armrests supplied as separate parts change the maintenance bill over time. Avoid tops bonded permanently to the base: one deep scratch then means removing the whole table.

Bar and circulation spine: what fails first

Around the bar, dimensions start from the counter. For a counter at 1,050–1,100 mm, a stool sets at 750–780 mm seat height, with a footrest around 450 mm. Allow 250–300 mm between stools, a 300–350 mm counter overhang for the knees, and 1,200 mm clear behind occupied stools — more where a stool pulls back. Without that overhang, knees meet the face of the counter, the guest sits at an angle and the seat edge wears. The main spine stays at 1,200 mm, widening to 1,500 mm at crossings, in front of the lifts and at the restaurant entrance. Low furniture — consoles, planters, display stands — belongs outside that spine, at least 300 mm from door leaves. Floor changes need a flat profile: a poorly set threshold bar trips guests and tears the glides off stools. The bar stool is the most-replaced item in a lobby: seat edge, seams, glides, footrest. Technical vinyl at 100,000 Martindale, a moulded seat with no seam across the front and a metal frame with screwed glides last better than furnishing fabric. Finally, separate quantities by zone and wear parts in the tender pack: seats, covers, tops, glides. That separation is what allows one batch of stools to be replaced without touching the waiting chairs, and keeps a lobby coherent after several years of trading.

Frequently asked questions

How much clear width does a hotel lobby need for circulation?

Allow 1.20 m clear on the main spine between entrance, reception and lifts, and 1.50 m at crossings, in front of the queue and at the restaurant entrance. Measure between furniture rather than between walls, and check it with the chairs pushed back.

How do you choose an armchair for a hotel lobby waiting area?

Start with dimensions: 420–450 mm seat height, 480–520 mm depth, 600–700 mm per place, and 800–900 mm between chairs facing each other. Then confirm foam at 35–40 kg/m³ in the seat, a removable cover and a fabric of 40,000 to 50,000 Martindale.

Which fabric holds up best in a busy hotel lobby?

Technical vinyl or coated fabric at 100,000 Martindale for bar stools, and 40,000 to 50,000 for waiting seats. Avoid pale shades at contact points, and ask for the supplier test report rather than relying on the label.

How should circulation be organised between reception and the bar?

Keep a direct 1.20 m spine from entrance to desk to lifts, widening to 1.50 m at crossings. Keep low furniture out of that spine and at least 300 mm from door leaves, and set the queue 1.50 m back from the desk.

Which spare parts should be planned for a hotel lobby?

Seat covers and cushions, glides and feet, table tops, desk kick plates and footrests. Ask for them as separate lines in the tender pack: that is what allows one wear part to be replaced without taking a whole seating group out of service.
